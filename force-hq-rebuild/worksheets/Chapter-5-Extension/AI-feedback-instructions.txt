HOW TO MARK
- Student answers are printed in blue. Mark only what the student added. The worksheet's own text, choices and word lists are not student work.
- Students don't need to match the answer key word for word: accept any correct scientific wording.
- The feedback page at the end of each PDF shows how the student did in the sim. If it disagrees with what you can see on the worksheet, trust the visible answer and say so.
- The worksheet starts with "Steps completed in the sim". A blue tick means the sim recorded that step as done. These ticks are not marked, but mention any skipped steps in the feedback.
- Auto-marked questions in the sim allowed one go each, so mark the answer exactly as printed.
- A blank question scores 0. Add a kind note saying what is missing.
- Some questions ask "How sure are you?" (Guessing, Fairly sure or Certain). Confidence is not marked. If a student was Certain but wrong, mention it kindly in the feedback: it shows an idea worth talking about.
- Don't take marks off for spelling unless the meaning is unclear.
- Mark each worksheet separately. An optional worksheet that wasn't submitted is not counted.

=== Force HQ Ch5 Extension (Falling and terminal velocity) worksheet (16 marks) · MYP: A ii, A iii, D i, D iii ===
Q1 Calculate, 2 marks [A ii]: 992 N (accept 991 to 993). 1 mark for the answer with its unit (N), 1 for correct working: weight = 40 × 24.8 = 992 N, more than two and a half times its weight on Earth..
Q2 Answer and reason, 2 marks [A ii]: 1 mark for the answer: They hit the ground at the same moment. 1 mark for the reason: There is no air, so only weight acts, and both gain 1.6 m/s of velocity every second. On the Moon there's no air resistance. Every kilogram is pulled with 1.6 N, so every object speeds up at the same rate. Astronaut David Scott did exactly this on Apollo 15 in 1971. Confidence is not marked.
Q3 Sketch the graph, 4 marks, 1 per check [A ii]: Starts at zero; Speeds up all the way; Speeds up by the same amount every second; Never levels off. a straight line rising from zero, because with no air the resultant force is the weight all the way down. The key shows the right graph.
Q4 Work it out, then order, 5 marks [A ii, A iii]: Titan 56 N; The Moon 64 N; Mars 148 N; Venus 356 N; Earth 392 N; Jupiter 992 N (accept within 0.5 N). Correct order: 1st B (Titan), 2nd A (The Moon), 3rd D (Mars), 4th E (Venus), 5th C (Earth), 6th F (Jupiter). Calculations, 3 marks: all 6 right = 3; 4 or more = 2; 2 or more = 1; otherwise 0. Order, 2 marks: 2 if every letter is right; 1 if only two neighbouring letters are swapped. Error carried forward: also give the 2 order marks if the student's order matches their own calculated values, even when one calculation was wrong.
Q5 Explain it, 3 marks, 1 per point [A ii, D i, D iii]: The question gives the words to use (thin air, air resistance, terminal velocity, weight); each point is about using one of them correctly. • Says the air on Mars is very thin, so there is much less air resistance • Says the air resistance only balances the weight at a high velocity, so the terminal velocity is still too high • Says rockets give an extra upward force that slows the rover down Model answer: Mars has very thin air, so the air resistance on the rover is much smaller than it would be on Earth at the same velocity. The air resistance only balances the rover's weight at a very high velocity, so even with a parachute its terminal velocity is still far too fast for a safe landing. Rockets add an extra upward force to slow it down.
