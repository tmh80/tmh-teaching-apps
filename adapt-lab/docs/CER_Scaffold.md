# CER and data skills in Adapt Lab

Draft for Tom, written 15 Nov 2026. This is the design for the CER writer and the data tools named in section 7 of `Adapt_Lab_Rebuild_Plan.md`. A later session builds from it. Every number here is an example: when the simulator is built, re-check each one against real runs.

Grade 6, students working alone. Nothing on a student screen mentions MYP strands.

## 1. What claim, evidence and reasoning mean (student words)

This is the card students see the first time, and can reopen from the Journal.

> **A CER paragraph answers a question in three parts.**
>
> **Claim.** One sentence that answers the question. It says what happened.
>
> **Evidence.** Numbers from your own tables and graphs that show your claim is true. Say what each number measures.
>
> **Reasoning.** The science idea that explains why your evidence fits your claim. It says why it happened.

A quick way to remember it, shown under the card:

| Part | It answers… | It usually starts… |
|---|---|---|
| Claim | What happened? | "The population…", "Coats became…" |
| Evidence | How do you know? | "The average went from … to …" |
| Reasoning | Why did it happen? | "This happened because…" |

### The worked example used all through this document

A student ran snow hoppers (a small hare-like ancestor) in the Arctic tundra for one era of 20 generations and typed these readings into their own table.

| Generation | Population | Average coat thickness (out of 100) |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 20 | 40 |
| 5 | 14 | 46 |
| 10 | 19 | 55 |
| 15 | 27 | 63 |
| 20 | 34 | 68 |

Deaths in the era, from the era report: too cold 96, starved 71, caught by predators 48, old age 22. Deaths from cold were 41 in generations 1–5 and 9 in generations 16–20.

**Question:** What happened to coat thickness in the snow hopper population, and why?

> **Claim.** Thick coats became more common in the snow hopper population.
>
> **Evidence.** The average coat thickness went up from 40 at generation 0 to 68 at generation 20. Deaths from cold fell from 41 in the first five generations to 9 in the last five.
>
> **Reasoning.** The hoppers were born with different coat thicknesses. Hoppers with thicker coats were more likely to survive the winter and have young. Their young inherited thick coats, so thick coats spread through the population. This is natural selection. No single hopper changed its own coat.

## 2. The CER checklist (the same six points in every stage)

Each part is worth 2 points, so a full CER is worth 6. This follows the skill's explain-question rule of one point per checklist point.

| Part | Point | How it is checked |
|---|---|---|
| Claim | **C1.** My claim answers the question. | Self-tick against the model |
| Claim | **C2.** My claim is one clear sentence, with no "because" in it. | Self-tick |
| Evidence | **E1.** I used at least two numbers from my own table, graph or era report. | The simulator checks this (section 4) |
| Evidence | **E2.** I said what each number measures, and which generation or run it is from. | Self-tick |
| Reasoning | **R1.** My reasoning uses a science idea to explain why. I used the words given correctly. | Self-tick; the sim shows which words it found |
| Reasoning | **R2.** My reasoning is about the whole population over many generations. It does not say an animal changed itself, or changed because it needed to. | Self-tick, after a nudge (section 5) |

After the student submits, the CER locks. The simulator shows the model CER beside theirs, the evidence it found, the words it found, and the "Did you include…?" list to tick. "Edit my answer" reopens it, as in Tilt Lab.

On the worksheet the three parts print in three labelled boxes, with the ticked checklist on the feedback page. The marking scheme lists the six points as rows.

## 3. The five stages (the support fades)

| Stage | Chapter | The student does | Points |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3 | Sorts six mixed sentences into Claim, Evidence, Reasoning | 6 |
| 2 | 4 | Picks the claim, fills evidence gaps with their own numbers, picks the reasoning | 6 |
| 3 | 5 | Writes all three parts from sentence starters | 6 |
| 4 | 6 | Writes all three parts with words to use only | 6 |
| 5 | 7 | Writes in a blank box, then marks a made-up student's CER | 6 + 6 |

### Stage 1 (Chapter 3): sort the sentences

**On screen:** "Mia ran two animals in the forest. Both freeze when a predator comes. One is brown and one is orange. She wrote a CER, but her sentences got mixed up. Put each sentence in the right box." Six cards and three boxes. Cards move by dragging or by tapping a card and then a box.

**Question on the card:** Which animal is safer from predators when it freezes: the brown one or the orange one?

| Card | Sentence | Box |
|---|---|---|
| a | The brown animal is safer from predators than the orange animal. | Claim |
| b | After 20 generations there were 41 brown animals but only 6 orange animals. | Evidence |
| c | Predators caught 22 brown animals and 87 orange animals. | Evidence |
| d | Freezing only works if a predator cannot see you. | Reasoning |
| e | Brown blends in with the leaf litter, so a brown animal that freezes is hard to spot. | Reasoning |
| f | Orange stands out, so an orange animal that freezes is easy to find and catch. | Reasoning |

Rules for these cards: every sentence is about the same length, the claim has no number in it, and both evidence cards have numbers and say what they count.

**Marking:** sort, 1 point per card, one go. Wrong cards get a green "→ correct box" tag.

**Feedback after the check:**
- A reasoning card put in Evidence: "Evidence has numbers you measured. This sentence explains why. That makes it reasoning."
- An evidence card put in Claim: "A claim answers the question in one sentence. This sentence is a measurement that backs it up."
- The claim put in Reasoning: "This sentence says what happened. Reasoning says why."

**Did you include…? (shown as a summary, not ticked at this stage):** "A claim says what happened. Evidence has numbers. Reasoning says why."

### Stage 2 (Chapter 4): pick, fill, pick

**On screen:** the question, then three small tasks in one card.

**Question:** What happened to coat thickness in your snow hopper population over 20 generations, and why?

**1. Pick the claim** (choose one):
- Thick coats became more common in the population. ✓
- Thin coats became more common in the population.
- Coat thickness stayed the same in the population.

Only one of these fits the student's own data, and the simulator marks against their data. If a student's run really did show no change (rare), the third option is correct for them.

**2. Fill the evidence with your own numbers** (four gaps):

> The average coat thickness was ____ at generation 0 and ____ at generation 20. Deaths from cold were ____ in the first five generations and ____ in the last five.

Each gap is checked against the student's own reading table and era report (section 4). A "📋 See my table" button opens the Journal beside the card.

**3. Pick the reasoning** (choose one; all four are about the same length):
- Hoppers born with thicker coats survived more often and passed thick coats to their young. ✓
- Each hopper grew a thicker coat during its life because the winters were so cold. (an individual changes in its lifetime)
- The hoppers needed thicker coats to survive, so their bodies made the coats thicker. (changing because of need)
- Thick coats are stronger than thin coats, so stronger hoppers always replace weaker ones. (evolution means stronger)

**Marking:** claim 1, evidence 1 per gap (4), reasoning 1. Total 6. One go for each of the three tasks.

**Feedback:**
- Wrong claim: "Look at your table. The average coat thickness went from 40 to 68. Which claim fits that?"
- An evidence gap that does not match: "Your table says 68 at generation 20. Copy the number from your own table."
- Reasoning "grew a thicker coat during its life": "Check the hopper you followed in Step 2. Its coat stayed at 38 all its life. The population changed because of who survived and had young."
- Reasoning "needed": "Needing something cannot change a body. Thin-coated hoppers needed thick coats too, and they died. The thick coats were already there in some hoppers."

After the check, the full model CER appears with the student's numbers dropped into it, so they see their first complete CER in their own data.

### Stage 3 (Chapter 5): sentence starters

**On screen:** three boxes, each with a starter the student keeps or changes, and word chips above the Reasoning box.

**Question:** Which change was more dangerous for your Arctic hares: slow warming or sudden warming?

| Box | Starter | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Claim | "The … warming was more dangerous because the population …" → changed to: "The … warming was more dangerous for the hares." | 150 characters |
| Evidence | "In the slow run, the population was … at generation … and … at generation … . In the sudden run, the population was … at generation … ." | 350 characters |
| Reasoning | "This happened because …" | 400 characters |

Note for the builder: the claim starter must not contain "because". The first version in the table above is the wrong one, kept here to show the mistake; use the second.

**Words to use (chips above Reasoning):** variation, survive, generations, inherit, natural selection, extinct.

**Example (student numbers):** slow run population 30 at generation 20, 17 at generation 28, 26 at generation 40. Sudden run 30 at generation 20, 0 at generation 23.

> **Claim.** The sudden warming was more dangerous for the hares.
>
> **Evidence.** In the slow run, the population fell from 30 at generation 20 to 17 at generation 28, then rose again to 26 by generation 40. In the sudden run, the population fell from 30 at generation 20 to 0 at generation 23.
>
> **Reasoning.** This happened because natural selection needs survivors and time. In the slow run, a few hares with thinner coats survived each generation. Their young inherited thin coats, so over many generations the population changed. In the sudden run, almost every hare died in one generation. There was no variation left to select from, so the population went extinct.

**Marking:** the six checklist points, 1 each. E1 is checked by the simulator; the student ticks the other five against the model.

### Stage 4 (Chapter 6): words to use only

**On screen:** three empty boxes labelled Claim, Evidence, Reasoning. No starters. Word chips above the Reasoning box. A small "Remind me what goes in each box" link reopens the card from section 1.

**Example question:** What happened to the Arctic foxes when the lemmings were removed from the tundra food web, and why?

**Words to use:** food web, consumer, energy, population, prey, knock-on effect.

**Example data (population index, 100 = normal, typed by the student from the food web chart):** Arctic fox 100 at year 0, 62 at year 10, 38 at year 20. Snowy owl 100, 55, 31. Arctic hare 100, 84, 71.

> **Claim.** The Arctic fox population fell when the lemmings were removed.
>
> **Evidence.** The Arctic fox index went from 100 at year 0 to 38 at year 20. The Arctic hare index also fell, from 100 to 71, even though nothing removed the hares.
>
> **Reasoning.** Foxes are consumers that get their energy by eating prey such as lemmings. With no lemmings there was less energy for the foxes, so fewer survived and bred. The foxes that were left ate more hares, which is a knock-on effect through the food web.

**Marking:** the six checklist points.

### Stage 5 (Chapter 7): on your own, then be the marker

**Part A, on screen:** one large box with no labels and no word chips. "Write a CER paragraph that answers the question. Use your own data." The simulator still runs the evidence check and still shows the model and the checklist afterwards.

**Example question:** You had 10 points to spend on conservation actions for the Arctic fox. Was your plan a good one?

**Part B, on screen:** "Sam wrote this CER. Use the checklist to mark it." The student ticks or crosses each of the six points, then taps the one sentence that needs fixing most and rewrites it.

Sam's CER (built to have two faults):

> My plan was good. The foxes did well because I helped them. There were lots of foxes at the end. A protected area stops habitat loss, so the foxes had more space and food and more of their young survived.

| Point | Correct mark | Why |
|---|---|---|
| C1 | ✓ | "My plan was good" answers the question |
| C2 | ✓ | One sentence, no "because" |
| E1 | ✗ | There are no numbers |
| E2 | ✗ | "Lots of foxes" does not say how many or when |
| R1 | ✓ | It explains how a protected area helps, using a science idea |
| R2 | ✓ | It is about the population |

**Marking Part B:** 1 point per correct tick or cross (6). The rewrite is an explain item with a two-point checklist: the new sentence has at least one number, and it says what the number measures.

## 4. The evidence check: how the simulator knows a number is really theirs

The aim is to teach students to go back to their data, not to catch them out. The check never blocks a student from handing in.

### What counts as "their data"

The simulator keeps a list of **recorded values** for each student. A value joins the list only when the student has had it on their own screen and written it down:
1. A cell in a reading table that the student typed and the simulator ticked as read correctly.
2. A cell the student typed into a table made with the table maker.
3. A number on an era report or snapshot the student saved to the Journal's Runs tab.

Each recorded value stores: the number, what it measures (population, average coat thickness, deaths from cold…), its unit if it has one, the generation or year, and the run name ("Run 3: snow hoppers, tundra").

Numbers the student never looked at do not count, even if the simulator knows them.

### Reading the student's sentence

1. Find every number in the evidence text: digits, decimals, numbers with a comma, percentages, and the words "zero" to "twenty", "half", "double", "twice".
2. A number that comes straight after "generation", "gen", "year", "era", "run" or "step" is a **label**, not evidence. It is used to find the right row. It never counts towards the two numbers, and it is never called wrong.
3. Every other number is tested against the recorded values.

### When a number matches

| Kind of quantity | A typed number matches when… |
|---|---|
| Counts (population, deaths) under 100 | it is within 1 of the recorded value |
| Counts of 100 or more | it is within 2% of the recorded value |
| Averages and percentages | it is within 1 of the recorded value (so 67.6 can be written as 68 or 67) |
| With "about", "around", "nearly", "almost", "roughly" in front | it is within 10% of the recorded value, or it is the recorded value rounded to the nearest 5 or 10 |

A number also matches if it is **worked out from two recorded values of the same quantity**:
- a difference ("rose by 28", from 68 − 40), within 1;
- a "times" or fraction ("doubled", "half as many", "three times"), within 10%;
- a percentage change, within 2 percentage points.

If a number matches more than one recorded value (20 could be a population or a death count), the simulator chooses the one whose quantity word is in the same sentence ("population", "coat", "deaths"). If there is no such word, it chooses the newest run.

### What the student sees

While the student types, small chips appear under the Evidence box:
- Green: "✓ 68 · average coat thickness at generation 20 · Run 3". Tapping it shows the row in the Journal.
- Grey: "? 75 · I can't find this in your runs yet".

A green chip that picked the wrong row has a small "Not this one" button, which lists the other matches.

When the student presses Submit:
- **Two or more green chips:** E1 is ticked. "You used 2 numbers from your own data."
- **One green chip:** "You have one number from your data. Good evidence usually shows a change or a comparison, so it needs at least two. Want to add one?" with "📋 See my tables" and "Hand in anyway".
- **No numbers at all:** "Your evidence has no numbers yet. Evidence is what you measured. Open your table and choose two numbers that show your claim is true." with the same two buttons.
- **A number it cannot find:** "I can't find 75 in your runs. Your table for Run 3 says 68 at generation 20. Check the number, or run it again and record it." with the same two buttons. The message never says the student made it up.

"Hand in anyway" always works. E1 is then left unticked, and the worksheet's feedback page says "Numbers found in the student's own data: 1 of 2".

### Units

Most Adapt Lab quantities are counts or scores out of 100. Temperatures have °C and survival chances have %. A matching number with a missing or wrong unit still matches. The simulator adds one gentle line: "Add what the number measures: 40 what?" This feeds checklist point E2, which the student ticks.

### Not punishing honest work

- Rounding is always accepted (the table above).
- A student who reads a graph by eye can use "about", which widens the match.
- A student who ran the experiment again can use numbers from any of their runs, as long as the sentence fits one run. If the two numbers come from different runs and the sentence compares them as one run, the chips show both run names so the student can see it.
- Teacher mode skips the check.

### Tests for the builder

Feed the checker these sentences with the worked example's data loaded. Expected green chips are in brackets.
- "The average coat thickness went from 40 at generation 0 to 68 at generation 20." (40, 68; the 0 and 20 are labels)
- "Coat thickness rose by 28." (28, as a difference: 1 chip, so the "one number" message)
- "The population nearly doubled, from 20 to 34." (20, 34)
- "About 100 hoppers died of cold." (100, matching 96 through "about")
- "The coats went from 40 to 75." (40; 75 not found)
- "Coats got thicker in generation 20." (none: 20 is a label)
- "There were lots more hoppers at the end." (none: no numbers)

## 5. Common weak CERs, and what the simulator says

These nudges appear before the student submits, as one line under the box they belong to. Each asks a question. None of them blocks.

| Weak CER | How the simulator spots it | The nudge |
|---|---|---|
| The evidence repeats the claim ("The coats got thicker.") | The Evidence box has no numbers | "This says what happened, like your claim. What did you measure that shows it? Add numbers from your table." |
| Evidence with no numbers ("There were lots more thick coats.") | No numbers, or words such as lots, many, more, most, a few with no number near them | "How many is 'lots'? Find the number in your table." |
| Numbers with no meaning ("It went from 40 to 68.") | Numbers match, but no quantity word is in the sentence | "40 what? Say what the number measures and which generation it is from." |
| Only one number | One green chip | "One number cannot show a change. Add a second number to compare it with." |
| The reasoning repeats the claim | More than 70% of the main words in the Reasoning box are also in the Claim box | "This says what happened again. Reasoning says why. Which science idea explains it?" |
| The reasoning has no "why" | No joining word such as because, so, this means, which, therefore | "Try starting with 'This happened because…'" |
| "Because it needed to" | Any of: needed to, need to, wanted to, tried to, decided to, in order to, so that it could, had to (with an animal as the subject) | "Can needing something change an animal's body? Think about the animals that died. They needed it too. What was different about the ones that lived?" |
| One animal changes itself | "grew", "became", "got", "developed" or "changed its" with a single animal as the subject ("the hare grew", "it grew", "each hopper") | "Did any single animal change during its life? Look at the animal you followed. Write about the population over many generations." |
| "Stronger" or "better" with no environment | stronger, better, best, improved, more advanced, with no biome or pressure word near it | "Better at what, and where? A thick coat helps in the tundra and costs energy in the desert." |
| A claim with the reason inside it | The Claim box contains because, so that, since | "Keep the claim to what happened. Move the 'because' part down to Reasoning." |

The last four nudges also appear on the feedback page for Tom when the student handed in without changing the sentence, under the heading "Ideas to talk about".

## 6. The graph and table skills ladder

Seven tasks, in order. Each chapter teaches one or two in Learn, with a hint ladder, before a Check uses it.

| Rung | Skill | First taught |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Read one value from a graph | Chapter 1 |
| 2 | Find the highest and lowest | Chapter 1 |
| 3 | Describe a trend in a sentence | Chapter 3 Foundation |
| 4 | Compare two lines | Chapter 3 Extension, again in Chapter 5 |
| 5 | Choose the right kind of graph | Chapter 4 Extension |
| 6 | Build the graph from your own table | Chapter 4 Extension |
| 7 | Use the graph as evidence | Chapter 5 |

### Rung 1: read one value

**Task:** a bar chart, "Deaths in one era by cause": too cold 96, starved 71, caught by predators 48, old age 22. "How many snow hoppers starved?" The student taps the bar, then types the number.
**Type:** read it. **Answer:** 71 (accept 70–72 when read by eye).
**Typical mistakes and feedback:**
- Reads the wrong bar (96): "That is the 'too cold' bar. Find the bar labelled 'starved' along the bottom first."
- Reads between grid lines badly (75): "The top of the bar is just above 70. Put your finger on the top of the bar and slide across to the numbers at the side."

### Rung 2: highest and lowest

**Task:** the same chart. "What was the biggest cause of death? What was the smallest?" Two drop-downs, then "How many more hoppers died of cold than of old age?"
**Type:** choose the words, then calculate. **Answers:** too cold; old age; 96 − 22 = 74.
**Typical mistakes and feedback:**
- Picks the first bar instead of the tallest: "The biggest cause has the tallest bar, not the first one."
- Adds instead of subtracting (118): "'How many more' means the difference. Take the smaller number away from the bigger one."

### Rung 3: describe a trend

**Task:** a line graph of average coat thickness against generation (40, 46, 55, 63, 68). First with drop-downs, then typed.
> "From generation **0** to generation **20**, the average coat thickness **went up / stayed the same / went down**, from **40** to **68**."

Then, unaided: "Write one sentence that describes what happened to the population." (20, 14, 19, 27, 34: it fell at first, then rose.)
**The trend sentence pattern, shown as a card:** *From ___ to ___, the ___ went ___, from ___ to ___.*
**Typical mistakes and feedback:**
- Describes one point ("It was 68"): "That is one reading. A trend says how it changed from the start to the end."
- No numbers ("It went up"): "How far did it go up? Add the first and last numbers."
- Misses a change of direction: "Look at the first five generations. Did it go the same way all the time?"
- Mixes up the axes ("the generations went up"): "The line shows coat thickness. Generation is just how far along we are."

### Rung 4: compare two lines

**Task:** one graph, two lines: population of brown animals and population of orange animals over 20 generations (brown 20, 24, 31, 37, 41; orange 20, 15, 11, 8, 6).
1. "Which line is the brown animals?" (tap the line; teaches reading the key)
2. Complete a trend grid: rows = brown, orange; columns = generations 0–10, 10–20; cells = goes up / stays the same / goes down.
3. "At generation 20, how many more brown animals were there than orange animals?" (41 − 6 = 35)
4. "Write one sentence that compares the two populations."

**Model sentence:** "The brown population went up from 20 to 41, but the orange population went down from 20 to 6."
**Typical mistakes and feedback:**
- Describes only one line: "You described the brown line. A comparison needs both. Use 'but' or 'while'."
- Reads the wrong line: "Check the key. Which colour is the orange animals?"
- Compares at different generations: "Compare them at the same generation, so the test is fair."

### Rung 5: choose the right kind of graph

**Task:** two small tables side by side.
- Table A: generation (0, 5, 10, 15, 20) and average coat thickness.
- Table B: cause of death (too cold, starved, predators, old age) and number of deaths.

"Which table needs a line graph? Which needs a bar graph?" Then the rule as a key idea box: **numbers along the bottom → line graph (scatter with joined points). Names along the bottom → bar graph.**
**Type:** match it up, then choose one on the key idea.
**Typical mistakes and feedback:**
- Bar graph for Table A: "Generation is a number that keeps going. A line shows how something changes as the number goes up."
- Line graph for Table B: "'Starved' and 'old age' are names. There is nothing between them, so a line joining them means nothing. Use bars."

In the graph maker, choosing scatter when the bottom column has words gives the warning already built in Tilt Lab.

### Rung 6: build the graph from your own table

**Task:** in the graph maker, plot average coat thickness against generation from the student's own table.
The live checklist (from Tilt Lab) is the mark scheme, 1 point each:
1. The right kind of graph is chosen.
2. The title says what the graph shows ("Average coat thickness over 20 generations").
3. The label along the bottom has what it measures ("Generation").
4. The label up the side has what it measures and its scale ("Average coat thickness (out of 100)").
5. The numbers on each axis go up in equal steps, and all the data fits.
6. Every point is plotted.
7. Every point is in the right place.

**Typical mistakes and feedback:**
- No units or scale on a label: "Coat thickness in what? Add '(out of 100)' so a reader knows what 68 means."
- Uneven axis numbers (0, 5, 10, 20, 40): "The gaps between your numbers change. Each step must be the same size. Try going up in 10s."
- The axis is too short for the data: "Your biggest value is 68, but your axis stops at 50. Make the highest number bigger."
- The wrong column plotted (population in place of coat thickness): "Your title says coat thickness, but these points are your population numbers. Choose the coat thickness column."
- The columns swapped (generation up the side): "The thing you chose or counted along (generation) goes along the bottom. The thing you measured goes up the side."
- Points in the wrong place: the red rings show which ones; "Check the point at generation 10. Your table says 55."

"Suggest numbers for me" stays available, but using it leaves checklist point 5 unticked in the Check, where the skill is being tested. In Learn it is free.

### Rung 7: use the graph as evidence

**Task:** the student picks one of their own graphs as a piece of evidence and writes the caption "What does this show?" (160 characters), then quotes two values from it in the Evidence box of a CER.
**Model caption:** "The average coat thickness went up from 40 to 68 over 20 generations in the tundra."
**Marking:** explain, 2 points: the caption states a trend or comparison (not just the title again); the caption has at least one number from the graph.
**Typical mistakes and feedback:**
- The caption repeats the title ("Coat thickness graph"): "That is what the graph is called. What does it show? Use your trend sentence."
- A graph that does not fit the claim (population graph for a claim about coats): "Your claim is about coats. Does this graph show coats? Choose the graph that shows your claim is true."
- No numbers quoted: "Read two points off your graph and put them in your evidence."

## 7. What the builder needs from the data tools

- A reading table type where each row unlocks at the right moment, the student types the value, and a correct reading joins the recorded values list.
- Era reports and snapshots that add their numbers to the recorded values list only when the student saves them.
- Run names that are short and human ("Run 3: snow hoppers, tundra"), shown on every chip.
- The CER writer as one component with five modes (sort, pick-fill-pick, starters, words only, blank), all saving in the same shape so the worksheet prints any stage in the same three-box layout.
- The checker tests in section 4.
- Minutes for the time estimates: stage 1 approx. 3 min, stage 2 approx. 4 min, stages 3–5 approx. 6 min each, building a graph approx. 6 min.
