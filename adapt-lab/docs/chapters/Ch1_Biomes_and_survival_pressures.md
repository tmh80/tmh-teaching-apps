# Chapter 1 · Biomes and survival pressures

Draft script for Tom to read and correct (written 15 Nov 2026). Grade 6, students working alone. No code yet: a later session builds from this.

**How to read this script.** Text in quote blocks is what the student reads. "Do this" jobs are ticked off by the sim. Every auto-marked question gets one go, then locks and shows the right answer with the reason. Strand tags (A i, A ii, A iii) are for the teacher only and never appear on a student screen.

**Screens named in this chapter**
- **Biome explorer:** the six biomes as living pictures, with a season dial, a day and night switch and fact cards.
- **Living scene:** where a group of animals or plants is released and watched for one year.
- **Data tools:** the bar chart of what happened, a table the student types into, and the Journal (Runs, Notes, Vocab).

**The test animal.** This chapter uses one real animal, the **European rabbit** (from the temperate forest), as a "test visitor". Students release 20 rabbits into different biomes. The rabbit never changes. Only the biome changes. That keeps the first idea clean: a biome is hard or easy *for a particular living thing*.

**Notes for the builder (check before building)**
1. A test release is **20 animals, one year, no births**, with a **fixed seed** per species and biome, so every student sees the same numbers and the answer key can print them.
2. The bar chart after a release is **"What happened to the 20 rabbits?"**: one bar per cause of death (too cold, overheated, starved, dried out, caught by predators, could not breathe) plus "still alive". A taller bar means more animals. This is easier for Grade 6 than the old survival-percentage bars, where the *shortest* bar was the biggest danger.
3. Run the model for every release named below and check the story holds (rabbit: best in forest, mostly "too cold" in tundra, mostly "dried out" or "overheated" in desert, all "could not breathe" in the ocean). If the model disagrees, change the script, not the model. Then paste the real numbers into the tables marked *[model numbers]*.
4. Every number in a Check chart marked *[example numbers]* is made up to show the shape of the question. Replace with model output and re-check the answers.

## Foundation · Six biomes, different dangers (approx. 26 min)

### Key ideas
1. A **biome** is a large area with its own climate, plants and animals.
2. A **survival pressure** is anything in the environment that makes it hard to stay alive. Some pressures are non-living (cold, heat, too little water, salt). Some are living (predators, animals that eat plants).
3. Every biome has different pressures. So the same animal can do well in one biome and badly in another.

### Misconceptions targeted
- **M5 "Tough animals can live anywhere."** (A feature that helps in one place helps everywhere.) Targeted in Learn step 5 and Check questions 3 and 7.
- **"Snow means plenty of water."** In the tundra most water is frozen and cannot be drunk or taken up by roots. Learn step 1 and Check question 5.
- **"The animal changed."** When rabbits die in the tundra and live in the forest, nothing about the rabbit is different. Learn step 5 and Check question 6.

### Vocabulary, in the order it is introduced
biome → climate → environment → survival → test release → bar chart → survival pressure → predator → non-living pressure → living pressure

### Learn steps

**Step 1 · Look around (approx. 4 min)** · Screen: biome explorer

> Welcome to Adapt Lab! There are six **biomes** here. A biome is a big area of the world with its own weather, plants and animals. Have a look around before we start.

Do this:
1. Tap each of the six biomes and watch the picture. (Ticks when all six have been opened.)
2. Open the fact card for the **Arctic tundra**. Type the coldest day temperature. *(inline answer box: −26 °C)*
3. Open the fact card for the **Hot desert**. Type the hottest day temperature. *(inline answer box: 44 °C)*
4. Open your **Journal** and tap the **Runs** tab. This is where your results will be saved.

Your turn (after "Next: questions"):
- **Match it up** (3 rows): "Days reach 44 °C and there is almost no rain" ↔ Hot desert · "The ground below the surface is frozen all year" ↔ Arctic tundra · "The water is salty and waves batter the shore" ↔ Coastal ocean.
- **Key idea box** (stays open until answered):
  > **Key idea: a biome is a large area with its own climate, plants and animals.** The **climate** is the usual weather over many years.
  
  Question: *Which of these is a biome?* A hot desert stretching across a country ✔ · A rainy afternoon in the desert · A burrow dug under the desert sand. (Distractors: weather is not a biome; a home is not a biome.)

Hint ladder for jobs 2 and 3: (1) "The fact card has a temperature line near the top." (2) The line is outlined on the card. (3) "The card says 'Days from −26 °C to 10 °C'. The coldest is the lower number: −26."

**Step 2 · Where can a rabbit live? (approx. 6 min)** · Screens: biome explorer, living scene

> Meet the **European rabbit**. In real life it lives in the temperate forest. Let's send 20 rabbits to visit three biomes for one year. Where will they do best? Decide first, then test it.

Prediction (locks before any release): two drop-downs.
- "Most rabbits will still be alive after a year in the … (Arctic tundra / Hot desert / Temperate forest)."
- "Fewest rabbits will still be alive in the … (Arctic tundra / Hot desert / Temperate forest)."

Do this:
1. Release 20 rabbits into the **Temperate forest**. Watch the year go by. Type how many are still alive. *(inline box)*
2. Release 20 rabbits into the **Arctic tundra**. Type how many are still alive. *(inline box)*
3. Release 20 rabbits into the **Hot desert**. Type how many are still alive. *(inline box)*

Each release is saved to Journal → Runs with one sentence, for example: "You released 20 European rabbits into the Arctic tundra. After one year, 2 were still alive. Most died of cold." *[model numbers]*

Your turn:
- **Observation (choose the words):** "The rabbits did best in the (forest / tundra / desert). My prediction was (right / partly right / wrong)." The second drop-down is not marked.
- **Observation (read it):** "How many more rabbits survived in the forest than in the tundra?" Checked against the student's own two runs. Clue fold-out: "Find the two numbers in your Runs. Take the smaller from the bigger."
- **Tap the sentence that…** explains why the rabbits did badly in the tundra:
  1. "The tundra made it hard for rabbits to stay alive." ✔
  2. "The rabbits in the tundra were weaker than the others."
  3. "The rabbits did not try hard enough in the tundra."
  
  After a wrong tap: "All 60 rabbits were the same kind of rabbit. What was different each time?"
  > **Key idea: anything in the environment that makes it hard to stay alive is called a survival pressure.** Your **environment** is everything around you.

**Step 3 · Read the bar chart (approx. 6 min)** · Screen: data tools. *This is the chapter's data skill.*

> After every release you get a **bar chart**. It shows what happened to your 20 rabbits. Let's learn to read it. It is easier than it looks!

"How do I read this chart?" card (three short lines, with the parts of the chart highlighted one at a time):
1. The **title** says what the chart is about: *What happened to 20 rabbits in the Arctic tundra*.
2. Each **bar** is one thing that happened. The words under the bar tell you what.
3. The **height** of the bar tells you how many rabbits. Read across to the numbers at the side.

Do this (tundra chart on screen, table under it):
1. Type the height of each bar into the table. *(Reading table, 6 cells: Too cold · Starved · Caught by predators · Dried out · Overheated · Still alive. Each cell is checked: "Look again at the bar. Read across to the number at the side.")*
2. Add up your six numbers. Type the total. *(It must be 20. If not: "Every rabbit is in exactly one bar. Check your readings.")*

Your turn:
- **Tap the moment:** "Tap the tallest bar." Then **gap:** "The biggest survival pressure for rabbits in the tundra was ____." (accepts cold, the cold, too cold, freezing, low temperature)
- **Tap the moment:** "Tap the shortest bar that is not zero." Then **gap:** "The smallest pressure was ____."
- **Now on your own:** the desert chart appears. **Gap ×2:** biggest pressure, smallest pressure. No table this time.

Hint ladder for "biggest": (1) "Biggest pressure means the one that killed the most rabbits." (2) "Look for the tallest bar. Do not count 'Still alive'." (3) Worked example on the forest chart: "In the forest the tallest bar is 'Caught by predators', so predators were the biggest pressure there."

**Step 4 · Living and non-living pressures (approx. 5 min)** · Screens: biome explorer, living scene

> Some survival pressures come from living things. Some do not. Go to the savanna and watch what the rabbits have to deal with there.

Do this:
1. Release 20 rabbits into the **Savanna grassland** and watch for a hunter. *(Ticks when a chase has played.)*
2. Tap the animal that is hunting the rabbits. *(Opens a card: "Cheetah. A **predator** is an animal that hunts and eats other animals.")*
3. Type the height of the "Caught by predators" bar. *(inline box)*

Your turn:
- **Observation (read it):** "In which biome so far were the most rabbits caught by predators?" (drop-down of the biomes the student has run; checked against their runs)
- **Sort it** (6 cards → "Comes from living things" / "Does not come from living things"): Cheetahs hunting · Freezing cold · Almost no rain · Zebras eating the grass a plant needs · Salt in the water · Tall trees blocking the light. (Tall trees: living. Feedback: "The shade is a pressure for small plants, and it comes from the trees, which are alive.")
- **Key-word gap:** "A cheetah is a ________ because it hunts and eats other animals." (predator)

**Step 5 · Same rabbit, different biome (approx. 5 min)** · Screen: living scene

> One more visit. This time the rabbits go to the **Coastal ocean**. What do you think will happen? Lock in your answer first.

Prediction (choose one, locks): "Most of the rabbits will…" drown because they cannot breathe underwater ✔ · freeze because the water is cold · starve because there is no grass. (All three are sensible. Only the run settles it.)

Do this:
1. Release 20 rabbits into the **Coastal ocean**.
2. Type the height of the tallest bar, and its name. *(inline box and drop-down)*

Your turn:
- **Observation (choose the words):** "In the ocean the biggest pressure for a rabbit was (breathing / cold / food). My prediction was (right / wrong)."
- **Who's right?** Three students talk about the results.
  - Mia: "Rabbits are weak animals. That is why so many died."
  - Kenji: "The rabbits were the same every time. The biomes were different."
  - Lena: "The rabbits in the forest were stronger rabbits."
  
  Answer: Kenji. Reason (choose one): "All the rabbits were the same kind, so the difference must come from the biome" ✔ · "Forest rabbits are a tougher kind, so they lived longer than the rest" · "Rabbits are small, so they die more easily than bigger animals do".
- **Key idea box:**
  > **Key idea: every biome has different survival pressures. The same animal can do well in one biome and badly in another.**
  
  Question: "A camel lives well in the hot desert. In the Arctic tundra it would most likely…" struggle, because the pressures there are different ✔ · do well, because camels are tough animals · do well, because it can store food in its hump.

## Extension · A year in a biome (approx. 27 min)

### Key ideas
1. Survival pressures change through the year. Every biome has a **harsh season**, when staying alive is hardest.
2. The harsh season is not always winter. In the hot desert it is the summer. In the savanna it is the long dry season.
3. Plants face survival pressures too: too little water, too little light, frost or heat, and animals that eat them.

### Misconceptions targeted
- **"Deserts are hot all the time."** Desert nights and winters can be cold (5 °C on a winter night in this model). Learn step 3, Check questions 2 and 6.
- **"Winter is always the hardest time."** Learn step 2, Check questions 1 and 4.
- **"Plants only need water" / "plants do not have problems to solve."** Learn step 4, Check questions 3 and 5.

### Vocabulary, in the order it is introduced
season → harsh season → dry season → temperature range → grazer → shade → frost

### Learn steps

**Step 1 · A year in the tundra (approx. 6 min)** · Screens: biome explorer, living scene, data tools

> So far you have looked at a whole year at once. But a year is not the same all the way through. Turn the **season dial** in the tundra and see what changes.

Do this:
1. Turn the season dial through all four tundra seasons. Watch the picture.
2. Fill in the table under the picture as you go. *(Freeze-frame table: stopping the dial on a season unlocks that row. Student types the day temperature. Spring thaw −5 °C · Short summer 10 °C · Autumn −8 °C · Long winter −26 °C.)*

Prediction (locks): "Arctic hares live in the tundra all year. In which season will the most hares die?" Spring thaw / Short summer / Autumn / Long winter.

3. Release 20 **Arctic hares** into the tundra for one year. The bar chart now shows **deaths in each season**.
4. Type the height of each season's bar. *(Reading table, 4 cells) [model numbers]*

Your turn:
- **Observation (tap the moment):** "Tap the bar for the season when the most hares died."
- **Observation (choose the words):** "The most hares died in the (long winter / short summer). That was also the (coldest / warmest) season in my table."
- **Key idea box:**
  > **Key idea: every biome has a harsh season. That is the part of the year when it is hardest to stay alive.**
  
  Question: "The harsh season in the Arctic tundra is the…" long winter, when it is far below freezing ✔ · short summer, when the snow has melted · spring thaw, when the ground is wet.

**Step 2 · Is winter always the hard part? (approx. 5 min)** · Screen: biome explorer

> In the tundra, winter is the harsh season. Is that true everywhere? Make your guess for two more biomes, then check with the season dial.

Prediction (two drop-downs, locks): "The harsh season in the **Hot desert** is … (spring / summer / autumn / winter)." "The harsh season in the **Savanna grassland** is the … (wet season / long dry season)."

Do this:
1. Turn the season dial in the **Hot desert**. Type the day temperature in summer *(44 °C)* and in winter *(20 °C)*.
2. Turn the season dial in the **Savanna grassland**. Watch the grass. Tap the season when the grass turns brown and dry. *(Long dry season)*

Your turn:
- **Observation (complete the table):** rows Tundra, Desert, Savanna; column "Harsh season" (drop-downs: long winter / scorching summer / long dry season / wet season); column "Why it is hard" (too cold / too hot and dry / no rain, so little food and water).
- **Tap the sentence that…** tells you the harsh season is not always winter:
  1. "In the hot desert, the harsh season is the scorching summer." ✔
  2. "In the Arctic tundra, the harsh season is the long winter."
  3. "In the temperate forest, the harsh season is the cold winter."

Hint ladder for the savanna: (1) "The savanna is hot all year, so cold is not the problem." (2) "Watch what happens to the grass and the water hole as you turn the dial." (3) "In the long dry season there is no rain for months. Grass dries up, so there is little to eat or drink."

**Step 3 · Day and night in the desert (approx. 5 min)** · Screen: biome explorer

> Here is something that surprises a lot of people. Go to the hot desert in **summer** and switch between **day** and **night**.

Prediction (choose one, locks): "On a summer night the desert will be…" much cooler than the day ✔ · about the same as the day · even hotter than the day.

Do this:
1. Set the desert to summer. Type the day temperature and the night temperature. *(44 °C and 26 °C)*
2. Set the desert to winter. Type the night temperature. *(5 °C)*

Your turn:
- **Observation (calculate):** "How many degrees cooler is the summer night than the summer day?" 44 − 26 = **18 °C**. Clue fold-out: "You have two temperatures. Which sum tells you the gap between them?" The pop-out calculator is highlighted.
- **Key-word gap:** "The gap between the highest and lowest temperature is called the temperature ______." (range) Taught on a card first: "The **temperature range** is the gap between the hottest and the coldest."
- **Who's right?**
  - Aiko: "A desert is always hot. That is what desert means."
  - Tomás: "A desert is dry. It can be hot in the day and cold at night."
  - Priya: "A desert is hot in summer and frozen solid all winter."
  
  Answer: Tomás. Reason: "The readings show 44 °C by day but only 5 °C on a winter night" ✔ · "The readings show 44 °C by day and 44 °C on a winter night" · "The readings show 44 °C by day but −26 °C on a winter night".

**Step 4 · Plants have pressures too (approx. 6 min)** · Screens: living scene, data tools

> Animals can run from danger. Plants cannot move at all. So what makes life hard for a plant? Let's test one. This is a **leafy forest shrub**.

Prediction (locks): "I think most of the shrubs planted in the **hot desert** will…" dry out / be eaten / not get enough light.

Do this:
1. Plant 20 shrubs in the **Hot desert**. Watch one year. Type the height of the tallest bar and its name.
2. Plant 20 shrubs in the **Savanna grassland**. Type the height of the "Eaten by grazers" bar. *(Card on first use: "A **grazer** is an animal that eats plants, such as a zebra.")*
3. Plant 20 shrubs on the floor of the **Temperate forest**, under the trees. Type the height of the "Not enough light" bar. *(Card: "**Shade** is where something blocks the light.")*

Your turn:
- **Observation (complete the table):** biome → biggest pressure for the shrub (dried out / eaten by grazers / not enough light), read from the student's three charts. *[model numbers: builder, check each biome gives the expected tallest bar; if the forest floor does not, use a low herb for job 3]*
- **Tick all that apply:** "Which of these are survival pressures for a plant?" Too little water ✔ · Animals eating its leaves ✔ · Taller plants blocking its light ✔ · Frost in a cold winter ✔ · Having nowhere to run to ✗ (plants do not run; the question is what harms them).
- **Key idea box:**
  > **Key idea: plants face survival pressures too. Their pressures are water, light, frost or heat, and grazers.**
  
  Question: "A small plant on the forest floor gets little light because…" tall trees above it block the sun ✔ · the forest gets very little rain · the forest soil is full of salt.

**Step 5 · Compare two bar charts (approx. 5 min)** · Screen: data tools. *Data skill, one step further.*

> You can learn even more by putting two charts side by side. Here are your rabbits in the forest and in the desert.

Do this:
1. Open **Compare** and choose your forest run and your desert run.
2. Type the "Dried out" number for each biome. *(two inline boxes)*

Your turn:
- **Choose the words:** "'Dried out' was (bigger / smaller / the same) in the desert than in the forest. 'Caught by predators' was (bigger / smaller / the same) in the desert." Checked against the student's runs.
- **Gap:** "The biggest pressure in the desert was ____. The biggest pressure in the forest was ____."
- **Explain (two sentences, with a starter):** "Which biome was harder for the rabbits? Use one number from each chart." Starter: "The ______ was harder, because ___ rabbits were still alive there, but ___ in the ______." Words to use: still alive, biggest, pressure. Checklist: ☐ names the harder biome ☐ gives a number from each chart ☐ names the biggest pressure.
